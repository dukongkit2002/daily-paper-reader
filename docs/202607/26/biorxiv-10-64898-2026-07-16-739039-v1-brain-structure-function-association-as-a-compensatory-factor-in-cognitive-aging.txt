ABSTRACT 12 

Functional activity in response to increasing task difficulty (BOLD-modulation) in regions of the default mode 13 

network (DMN/task-negative) and multiple demand network (MDN/task-positive) shows age-related decline, 14 

which is associated with reduced cognitive performance. While BOLD-modulation in the DMN begins to decline 15 

in middle-age, white matter structural connectivity declines earlier in MDN regions. We conjecture that to 16 

maintain executive function (EF) performance with aging, altered BOLD-modulation in DMN during task 17 

engagement serves as a compensatory reaction to structural decline in MDN. To test this possibility, 18 

functionally-guided tractography was applied in 160 healthy adults aged 20-94 to locate white matter 19 

connecting MDN and/or DMN regions that were active during a distance judgement paradigm. Specifically, 20 

analyses examined the effects of 1) age on white matter tracts (fractional anisotropy; FA); 2) structure-function 21 

association between FA and BOLD-modulation; and 3) age, positive/negative BOLD-modulation, and quadratic 22 

FA on EF. Age-related decline was found in one MDN tract (U-shaped tract) and a significant structure-function 23 

association was found in inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF). Additionally, an interaction effect between 24 

quadratic frontal-insular (U-shaped) tract FA, age, and positive/negative BOLD-modulation was found for 25 

inhibition performance, supporting the hypothesis of compensatory functional activity effects on executive 26 

function for older adults with altered white matter microstructure. 27

1. INTRODUCTION 31 

Age-related decline of structural integrity and functional activity in the brain contributes to declines in 32 

working memory and executive functions (Brown et al., 2019; Hakun et al., 2015; Webb et al., 2019, 2020; Zhu 33 

et al., 2015). Functional neuroimaging studies have established that two opposing largescale, intrinsic brain 34 

networks contribute to higher-order cognitive functions such as executive functions and working memory. The 35 

multiple demand network (MDN) comprises brain regions that demonstrate task-related positive blood 36 

oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) activity, which increases as task difficulty increases. In contrast, the 37 

default mode network (DMN) demonstrates task-related negative BOLD activity, which becomes more 38 

negative-going as task difficulty increases (Grady et al., 2010). Across the adult lifespan, BOLD modulation to 39 

task difficulty weakens with older age in both networks and has been associated with decreased working 40 

memory (Kennedy et al., 2017; Rieck et al., 2017). The joint contribution (or coupling) of positive BOLD 41 

modulation (PosMod) and negative BOLD modulation (NegMod) has been linked to better cognitive control 42 

(Spreng et al., 2010; Spreng et al., 2016; Turner & Spreng, 2015), and higher fluid intelligence (Rieck et al., 43 

2017). 44 

Although age-related decreases in PosMod and NegMod have been well studied, albeit mainly 45 

independently, the mechanism underlying the linkage of functional dynamics in DMN and MDN with advanced 46 

aging is still unclear, as are its effects on cognition across the adult lifespan. One crucial factor affecting 47 

functional dynamics is structural integrity of the brain (Warbrick et al., 2017). Accumulating evidence has linked 48 

age-related decline of white matter (WM) structural connectivity (fractional anisotropy, FA) to task-related 49 

BOLD activity (Brown et al., 2019; Hakun et al., 2015; Webb et al., 2019, 2020; Zhu et al., 2015). In regions of 50 

the MDN, some studies have reported that age-related decline in FA is accompanied by “over-recruitment” of 51 

BOLD activity in older relative to younger adults during task performance (Burzynska, Garrett, Preuschhof, 52 

Nagel, Li, Backman, et al., 2013; Zhu et al., 2015). Longitudinal findings confirm this pattern, in that reduction 53 

of FA in the anterior corpus callosum over three years was associated with increased BOLD activity in the 54 

ventral lateral prefrontal cortex (Hakun et al., 2015). These results suggest a close association between 55 

regions of functional activation and the integrity of the WM fibers connecting them. Likewise, age-related 56 

reductions in FA of tracts inter-connecting DMN regions were associated with less DMN deactivation. Yet, 57

most findings demonstrating a relationship between “functional over-recruitment” and WM decline link it to 58 

worse behavioral performance (Brown et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2019; Hakun et al., 2015; Zhu et al., 2015). 59 

Whether this cross-modality structure-function association is merely a result of structural degeneration or a 60 

compensatory mechanism to offset declining cognition is therefore unclear. 61 

Considering that brain activation is more dynamic than brain structure, it is appealing to hypothesize that 62 

activation might dynamically change to compensate for degraded brain structure (Dasalaar et al., 2013; Davis 63 

et al., 2008). The notion of compensatory activation is not new (Cabeza, 2002; Grady, 2012; Reuter-Lorenz et 64 

al., 2000); rather, it is an influential theoretical concept in the cognitive neuroscience of aging, despite arguably 65 

insufficient direct empirical validation. To clarify the definition of “compensation” and distinguish it from 66 

previous conceptualizations, here we consider “structure-function compensation”, a process that, compared to 67 

its absence, results in significantly better cognitive outcomes in older adults. For example, increasing BOLD 68 

modulation would be associated with better cognitive scores for those with age-related degenerated WM tracts 69 

should that modulation be compensatory in nature. 70 

One factor must be considered before further interpretation of the effect of structure-function compensation 71 

on cognition: degradation of the structure (e.g., white matter) and the function (e.g., BOLD) of the same brain 72 

network does not necessarily occur simultaneously. Notably, structural connectivity between DMN areas 73 

evidences age-related declines at a slower (cross-sectional) pace than does structural connectivity in the 74 

multiple-demand network (Figley et al., 2015; Hoagey et al., 2019), whereas age-related decline in functional 75 

activity occurs earlier in the lifespan in the DMN than the MDN (Park et al., 2010; Park & Friston, 2013; Rieck 76 

et al., 2017). These findings are seemingly inconsistent with the previous directional assumption of “ loss of 77 

structural integrity, loss of function” in the DMN (Andrews-Hanna et al., 2019). Therefore, it is worth exploring 78 

whether the decline of functional activity in DMN in middle-aged adults is associated with degeneration of WM 79 

in the MDN. In other words, exploring the structure-function association between NegMod and FA of MDN-80 

related WM tracts. 81 

To better examine a compensation effect within and across networks, structural and functional data must 82 

be acquired separately from each network. While separating BOLD signals according to different brain 83 

networks is common, locating white matter tracts that connect the regions belonging to a specific brain network 84

Using this method, robust within-network structure-function associations have been reported in DMN and MDN 88 

(Brown et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2019), while studies applying anatomical atlases in 89 

tractography have reported inconsistent results (Webb et al., 2019, 2020). The integrity of the tracts connecting 90 

functional ROIs of interest might, therefore, be more sensitive to the functional activity adjacent to them (Gazes 91 

et al., 2020; Preti et al., 2014; Reid et al., 2017; Staempfli et al., 2008). 92 

Thus far, no study has examined structure-function compensation effects between the DMN and MDN and 93 

cognitive function. Therefore, the current study aims to test this account of compensation. We hypothesize that 94 

increased greater BOLD signal in response to increases in task difficulty is associated with degraded white 95 

matter integrity within the MDN, but is age-dependent (e.g., for middle-aged and older adults) and associated 96 

with better cognitive performance. To test these predictions, functionally-guided tractography was employed to 97 

locate structural connections between regions showing age-related BOLD modulation (see Rieck et al., 2017). 98 

We tested our hypotheses by (1) locating WM tracts connecting to a major hub in each network (insula for 99 

PosMod and precuneus for NegMod) and examining the tracts for age-related differences in microstructure; (2) 100 

for the tracts identified, testing whether there are age-related structure-function associations within and across 101 

the two networks; (3) examining whether and how age-related structure-function associations are associated 102 

with a measure of executive function. Three possible associations would qualify as a compensatory effect: 103 

within-network across modality compensation (MDN-sourced BOLD and FA; DMN BOLD and FA), between-104 

network across modality compensation (MDN BOLD and DMN FA; DMN BOLD and MDN FA), and/or coupled 105 

BOLD network modulation and tract FA from either network (MDN x DMN BOLD and FA from MDN or DMN 106 

tracts). 107

of BOLD modulation between the MDN and DMN in offsetting degenerated WM microstructure in the MDN. We 109 

hypothesized that WM tracts in the MDN would show significant negative age effects and, according to current 110 

evidence on structure, this degraded WM would be associated with weakened BOLD modulation in DMN for 111

2. METHODS 125 

2.1. Participants 126 

This cross-sectional study utilized a lifespan sample of 161 healthy adults from the first wave of our 127 

Dallas Area Lifespan Longitudinal Aging Study (D.A.L.L.A.S), the same sample of participants as described in 128 

Rieck et al. (2017). Participant ages ranged from 20-94 years (mean age = 51.93 ± 18.9 yrs; 59% women) and 129 

they were recruited from the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex. Participants were screened to be right-handed with 130 

normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. To screen against signs of dementia and depression, all 131 

participants underwent a Mini Mental Status Exam (MMSE) (Folstein et al., 1975) and obtained a score of at 132 

least 26 (mean MMSE = 29, range 26 - 30); they also completed the Center for Epidemiologic Study 133 

Depression Scale (CESD; Radloff (1977)) and were required to score less than or equal to 16 on the scale 134 

(mean CESD = 4.29, range 0 - 16). Participants were further screened against a history of neurological, 135 

cardiac, metabolic or psychiatric conditions, head injury with loss of consciousness > 10 min, or current 136 

psychotropic medication use. Participants completed two visits for neuropsychological and cognitive tests at 137 

the University of Texas at Dallas' (UTD) Center for Vital Longevity and an MRI session at the Advanced 138 

Imaging Research Center at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center (UTSW). The study was 139 

approved by the UTD and the UTSW Institutional Review Boards. Participants provided written informed 140

2.2. Cognitive Measures 146 

For the current study, three metrics of cognitive performance were selected as outcome measures. 147 

From the fMRI task (see section 2.3.2), mean accuracy of the distance judgments (averaged across conditions 148 

and across runs) was computed as an index of in-scanner task performance. As measures of executive 149 

function, switching and inhibition composites were computed from two subtests of the Delis-Kaplan Executive 150 

Function System (Delis et al., 2001) (D-KEFS): Verbal Fluency and Stroop Color-Word Interference tasks. 151 

Scores from the "Culture Fair Intelligence Test" (2007) (CFIT) served as an index of fluid intelligence. Total 152 

time taken for each task was about 10 – 12 minutes. Individual cognitive test details are described below. 153 

2.2.1. D-KEFS Verbal Fluency Category Switching task . This task contained three conditions: Letter 154 

Fluency, where participants generated as many words as possible in 60 seconds that begin with a specific 155 

letter; Category Fluency, which required participants to generate as many words as possible in 60 seconds 156 

belonging to a specified category (e.g. Animals). To suppress retrieving repeated and/or irrelevant responses, 157 

inhibition may be involved in these two conditions (Shao et al., 2014); and Category Switching, which required 158 

participants to switch back and forth between naming words belonging to one of two different specified 159 

categories (e.g., apple, chair, banana, desk, …etc) for 60 seconds. As an index of Inhibition, the mean number 160 

of correct responses for letter fluency and category fluency (3 letter trials, 2 category trials) was calculated. The 161 

index of Switching was the total correct score for the Category Switching condition. 162 

2.2.2. D-KEFS Color-Word Interference (Stroop) task . The Stroop task contained four conditions: 163 

Two control conditions included Color Naming speed and Word Reading speed, where participants either 164 

named the color of the patches or read the words printed in black. In the Inhibition condition, they were asked 165 

to name the color of the ink, regardless of the actual word. In the Inhibition/Switching condition, participants 166 

had to switch between naming the ink color or reading the word. Response time (seconds) to complete was 167

2.2.3. Fluid Reasoning: CFIT . The Cattell Culture Fair Intelligence Test (CFIT) is designed to evaluate 175 

fluid intelligence, particularly reasoning. The test utilizes matrix reasoning problems and consists of four 176 

subtests each targeting different aspects of fluid intelligence. Scale 3 and Form B were used in the current 177 

study, containing a total of 50 items across four subtests: 1) Series subtest (13 items) assesses the ability to 178 

recognize and extend patterns in a sequential manner; 2) Classification subtest (14 items) measures 179 

individual’s capacity to discern subtle differences and categorize objects. 3) Matrices subtest (13 items) 180 

evaluates the ability to identify missing elements; 4) Topology subtest (10 items) measures participants’ 181 

analogous and spatial abilities. Correct responses within the time limit were recorded across the four subtests 182 

and averaged to create a composite fluid intelligence score (CFIT score). 183

2.3.2. fMRI distance judgment task procedure . Participants performed in the scanner a spatial 194 

distance judgment task (Baciu et al., 1999; Park et al., 2010). In a blocked design, participants performed two 195 

types of judgments: (1) in the control condition, a horizontal bar appeared in the middle of the screen, 196 

accompanied with a dot on either the left or right side of the bar, and participants indicated whether the dot was 197 

on the left or right with a button press; (2) At the beginning of each task block, participants were cued with a 198 

vertical bar in the middle of the screen, which served as a reference of distance, for three seconds, then 199 

disappeared and was replaced by a horizontal bar and a dot either above or below it. Their task is to judge 200 

whether the dot was “nearer” or “farther” from the bar relative to the reference bar length. This judgment 201 

involved three levels of difficulty: easy, medium and hard, parametrically varying the distance between the dot 202 

and bar. The reference cue was only displayed before each block of trials and did not vary. Participants 203 

responded with their index or middle finger whether the dot was “farther”/ “nearer” or “left”/ “right” relative to the 204 

bar. Stimulus presentation time was 2500 ms with a 500 ms interstimulus interval. There were three runs with 205 

20 blocks in each run, and four conditions were presented in a pseudo-random order in each run. The total 206 

functional scan time was about 15 min. Mean accuracy and median response time (RT) for each task condition 207 

(easy, medium, hard) were reported in our previous publication (Rieck et al., 2017). For the present study, 208 

mean accuracy across task difficulty (easy, medium, hard conditions) was the index of performance. 209 

2.3.2.1. fMRI analysis . The current structure-function study used the previously processed, analyzed, 210 

and published functional clusters from the age-related BOLD modulation to difficulty effect reported in Rieck et 211 

al., (2017). Detailed image processing steps are included in our previous study and here we only include 212 

details pertinent to the current study. Increased and decreased activation in the Hard relative to the Easy 213 

distance judgment conditions was the contrast of interest. That contrast isolated voxels that increased or 214 

decreased their activation with increasing task difficulty, a process henceforth referred to as “BOLD 215 

modulation”. Increased activation to greater difficulty is referred throughout as “Positive BOLD Modulation”; 216 

decreased activation to greater difficulty is referred to as “Negative BOLD Modulation”. fMRI data were all 217 

transformed to MNI space (unlike the diffusion data described below, which were kept in native space). The 218 

second-level model included age as a mean-centered continuous predictor to isolate age effects on positive 219 

and negative BOLD modulation in a whole brain voxel-wise manner with a conservative height threshold p 220 

< .0001, and clusters accepted as reliable at an extent threshold of p < .05 FWE. Those resulting clusters that 221

2.3.3 DTI Preprocessing and Analysis. DTI data were preprocessed using the DTIPrep v1.2.4 quality 235 

control package. Diffusion tensors were computed using DSI Studio (Yeh et al., 2013). The nine grey matter 236 

functional clusters in Table 2 served as our functionally-informed ROIs for deterministic tractography 237 

(implemented in DSI Studio) to build WM tracts of interest traversing among these functional regions. To allow 238 

tractography to be performed in native diffusion space the fMRI clusters were non-linearly registered to each 239 

participant’s b0 diffusion image with the Advanced Normalization Tools package (Avants et al., 2009) using a 240 

registration of the MNI 1mm 3 template to each participant’s T1-w MPRAGE, followed by a non-linear 241 

registration between the T1-w MPRAGE and the b0 diffusion image using linear interpolation. Each functional 242 

cluster was warped from MNI template space to the participant’s diffusion space simultaneously using the 243 

linear and non-linear warps with nearest neighbor interpolation. These warped ROIs were then dilated to aid in 244 

tractography. The mean number of white matter voxels within these raw clusters was 1284 voxels. Each ROI 245 

was then dilated with a kernel size of 0–3, so that they all covered at least 1284 white matter voxels. 246

2.3.3.1. Deterministic tractography . To build white matter connections that were the most related to 247 

the positive BOLD modulation in MDN and negative BOLD modulation in DMN, we focused on the connections 248 

linked to the hub of the respective network: either the insula (cluster #2 from Table 2 ) or cuneus/precuneus 249 

(cluster # 8 from Table 2 ) for MDN and DMN, respectively (Bullmore & Sporns, 2009). All 15 possible cluster 250 

combinations were submitted to fiber-tracking in each participant. Deterministic tractography was performed for 251 

each participant; each tract generated was carefully visually inspected, and any grossly atypically appearing 252 

tracts were excluded from analysis. The DSI Studio tractography fitting parameters for each tract can be found 253 

in Supplemental Table 1 . To ensure a multivariate normal distribution of number of streamlines among all 254 

tracts, the Mahalanobis distance was calculated using the number of streamlines for each tract for each 255 

participant using R v4.1.0. Participants with extreme Mahalanobis distance on their streamlines (using pchisq 256 

in R, p < .001), indicating that the number of streamlines of one or more tracts was either extremely low or 257 

high, were categorized as outliers and removed from further analysis. In total, nine participants were removed 258 

for low streamline counts. 259

functionally-guided tracts were identified and are illustrated in Figure 2 . Two of these tracts resided within the 261 

PosMod-related network, identified in red in the figure. The first tract connected right posterior parietal cortex 262

2.4. Statistical approach 290 

To test the three study aims (detailed in each corresponding results section) General Linear Models 291 

(GLM) were specified and computed using R v4.1.0 (R core team, 2021). Quadratic age and FA terms were 292 

examined based on previous studies that reported quadratic effects of age on white matter microstructure 293 

(Slater et al., 2019). WBFA served as a covariate in all models. The two BOLD modulation variables were the 294 

mean of the contrast parameter estimates of the five PosMod-related clusters or the mean estimates of the 295 

four NegMod-related clusters. To examine the structure-function compensation effects on executive function 296 

and fluid intelligence (aim 3), we ran separate models employing age, FA, FA 2, positive/negative BOLD 297 

modulations, and their interactions as predictors of task accuracy, executive function, or fluid intelligence as 298 

the three dependent variables, respectively. 299

4. DISCUSSION 482 

The current study examined the hypothesis that structure-function associations can serve a 483 

compensatory role in cognitive aging. To explore these associations, both within- and between networks, 484 

we first located four white matter tracts connecting the hub of areas of the multiple demand network 485 

(MDN) and, separately, the default mode network (DMN). By incorporating structural and functional brain 486 

measures within the same model as predictors of cognitive performance, we were able to observe coupling of 487 

positive and negative BOLD modulation as compensatory support of executive function. In accordance with our 488 

hypotheses, this coupling effect was observed in middle-aged and older adults, when the white matter in the 489 

MDN began to decline. Most importantly, this coupling effect varied dynamically with the microstructural 490 

integrity of white matter as indexed by FA. 491 

These significant effects of structure-function association on executive function were most frequently 492 

observed for the frontal-insular U-fiber tract, a short-range superficial white matter bundle. Among the four 493 

white matter tracts generated by our functionally-guided tractography, the fronto-insular tract was the most 494 

vulnerable to age after controlling for the whole brain fractional anisotropy. Additionally, an age-dependent 495 

brain structure-function association was observed on another MDN tract (IFOF) and negative BOLD 496 

modulation. In contrast to our expectation, older adults with lower MDN FA demonstrated enhanced BOLD 497 

modulation in the DMN. Below, we discuss potential mechanisms of structure-function compensation across-498 

and within-networks as related to age-related decline, focusing on the U-fiber tract. 499 

4.1. Coupling of positive and negative BOLD modulation in compensating for cognitive aging 500 

The motivation for the present study was to explore whether the differential effects of age on 501 

structural connectivity and functional activity across the lifespan (i.e., white matter MDN decline occurs 502 

before functional changes in that network, but vice versa in the DMN, cross-sectionally) were related to 503 

cognitive performance. We hypothesized that weakened negative BOLD modulation might compensate 504 

for declined white matter connectivity in the multiple demand network to maintain performance. If the 505 

increased BOLD activity is associated with better executive function scores in people with lower white 506 

matter connectivity, then we defined it as a compensation effect. The results revealed that weakened 507 

negative BOLD modulation compensates for declined white matter in the frontal lobe and reveals a potential 508

reason for “overactivation” in the DMN with increased age. Age-related weakening of negative BOLD 509 

modulation or over-activation in DMN regions during task performance has been linked to poorer performance 510 

on switching (Basak et al., 2018; Nashiro et al., 2018), n-back (Prakash et al., 2012; Qin & Basak, 2020) or 511 

executive function tasks (Park et al., 2010; Persson et al., 2007). The weakened negative BOLD activity in 512 

response to increasing demand has been considered an example of inefficient deployment of neural resources 513 

in adults, associated with reduced cognitive control, and inhibitory deficits (Gazzaley et al., 2005). By contrast, 514 

weakened DMN during task performance has also been attributed to greater reliance on prior knowledge in 515 

support of goal-directed behavior (Turner & Spreng, 2015). This overactivation in DMN regions across all task 516 

difficulty levels would then reflect weakened negative BOLD modulation(Spreng et al., 2014), and could be an 517 

indication of a compensatory effect (Duda et al., 2019), or reduced dynamic range of network modulation to 518 

increasing task demand (Spreng & Schacter, 2012). The present results partially align with the default-519 

executive coupling hypothesis of aging (DECHA model) (Spreng & Turner, 2019; Turner & Spreng, 2015), 520 

which posits that older adults rely on stored existing knowledge or long-term memory representations during 521 

task performance. The current findings extend the DECHA model by providing evidence that engagement of 522 

default regions can benefit older adults’ cognitive performance. 523 

Previous research demonstrated that coupling of greater BOLD modulation in the MDN and DMN is 524 

linked to higher fluid intelligence in the same lifespan sample as that investigated in the present study (Rieck et 525 

al., 2017). The present findings extend those prior results by demonstrating that the coupling effect is mainly 526 

driven by participants with lower frontal-insular U-fiber tract FA (Figure 5d). For people with lower U-fiber tract 527 

FA, better reasoning performance is linked not only to stronger coupling of the two networks but also to the 528 

lower BOLD modulation in both networks. Moreover, the combination of MDN engagement and reduced DMN 529 

suppression has been linked to older age (Spreng & Turner, 2019; Turner & Spreng, 2015). While functional 530 

antagonism between the two networks is associated with cognitive control in healthy adults, age-related 531 

decrease in structural connectivity might result in weakened functional activity that lead to either worse 532 

behavioral performance or a (compensatory) better performance (Xia et al., 2022). Prior multimodal studies of 533 

structural and functional brain aging reported that associations between age and BOLD activation in the DMN 534 

were mediated by white matter integrity (Brown et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2018). Further, the same group 535 

postulated that age-related decline of white matter might create a noisy environment for functional activation, 536

which decreases the suppression of DMN activity and results in poorer cognitive performance. Similarly, 537 

increased functional activity in MDN-related regions may indicate reduced efficiency or failed compensation for 538 

structural decline (Brown et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2015). 539 

In contrast to the previous studies that link functional coupling of the MDN and DMN to poor cognition 540 

or decreased behavioral performance, by examining BOLD activity from both networks in the same model (i.e., 541 

examining independent and synergistic effects) we were able to show negative coupling of BOLD modulation 542 

between the DMN and MDN networks is associated with better executive function in older adults with degraded 543 

frontal white matter. One major difference between the current and previous studies is that the current study 544 

tested the quadratic effect of white matter integrity in association with these networks, while most studies 545 

examined only linear effects. It has consistently been reported that the effects of age on white matter integrity 546 

follow a quadratic or cubic function (Lebel et al., 2012; Slater et al., 2019). This nonlinear effect suggests that 547 

structural connectivity in MDN might play different roles in modulating the functional activity of the two networks 548 

with regard to cognitive performance for older adults. Depending on whether participants possess “high” or 549 

“low” FA, the dynamic of this coupling effect changes. 550 

While it is not completely understood how the short-range U-fiber tract connecting inferior frontal 551 

regions and insula affects functional activation in MDN and DMN, previous studies have demonstrated the 552 

causal influence of the right anterior insula on functional activity in the DMN (Chiong et al., 2013; Sridharan et 553 

al., 2008). Similarly, the integrity of the tract connecting the right insula and presupplementary motor 554 

area/dorsal anterior cingulate cortex is associated with functional connectivity between right insula and DMN 555 

(Jilka et al., 2014). In relation to the present quadratic effect (Figure 9), we propose that age-related decline in 556 

frontal U-fiber microstructure is accompanied by alteration in the dynamic coupling of BOLD modulation 557 

between the MDN and the DMN. Nevertheless, higher positive and lower negative BOLD modulation were 558 

associated with better executive function, and this negative coupling was especially evident in those with 559 

relatively high or low FA levels. It is possible that, among middle-aged adults in the early phase of U-fiber 560 

degradation, higher FA was associated with an increased capability of utilizing the DMN (increased activation) 561 

to compensate for behavioral performance. For older adults in a later phase of U-fiber degradation, those with 562 

relatively poor FA also demonstrated the same negative coupling effect, which was not observed among 563

4.2. Age-related decrease of white matter microstructure in the multiple demand network 568 

Of the four white matter tracts generated by functionally-guided tractography in the current study, two 569 

were MDN-related, one was DMN-related, and one connected both networks (cross-network). Consistent with 570 

our hypothesis that white matter connecting regions of the multiple demand system is more vulnerable to 571 

increasing age than white matter connecting regions of the default mode system, an age-related reduction in 572 

FA of the frontal-insular U-fibers was identified. It is important to note that this effect was above and beyond 573 

the general effects of age on global white matter FA. If whole brain FA was not modeled as a covariate of no 574 

interest, linear effects of age were observed across all tracts. Contrary to most studies showing a quadratic 575 

effect of age on white matter integrity (Lebel et al., 2012), a linear age effect was observed in the current 576 

study. One possible explanation is that these tracts are only partial streamlines connecting targeted regions 577 

rather than full tracts, and the linear age effect indicates that the streamlines relevant to executive function are 578 

susceptible to age as early as their 20s. 579 

However, including whole brain FA in the models is necessary to establish the specificity of the effects 580 

to the tracts of interest. The current finding supports previous longitudinal work demonstrating robust aging 581 

effects on FA in these short-range white matter tracts (Schilling et al., 2023) and is consistent with our prior 582 

finding (Hoagey et al., 2019). In light of the “first in last out” principle (Raz, 2000), it is not surprising to observe 583 

age effects on these small tracts, which have a protracted development and reach peak FA later in the lifespan 584 

than the longer-range association tracts (Wu et al., 2014). Furthermore, short-range fibers tend to be less 585 

myelinated than long-range fibers and hence may be more vulnerable to the deleterious effects of age (Gao et 586 

al., 2014). 587

activation in older adults with relatively low white matter FA, where greater BOLD modulation correlated with 589 

better performance on inhibition (Figure 6). Reduced white matter integrity in frontal-parietal regions in older 590

4.3. Cross-network structure-function association 605 

When considering structure-function associations alone, we found that the association between FA and 606 

negative BOLD modulation was modulated by age (Figure 4). For younger adults, higher IFOF FA was 607 

associated with greater negative BOLD modulation to task difficulty, while older adults demonstrated the 608 

opposite association (higher FA correlating inversely with negative modulation). The structure-function 609 

association finding in younger adults is consistent with previous studies exploring such associations within the 610

the MDN amplified its inhibitory effect on the DMN and, as a consequence, the MDN demonstrated greater 614 

negative BOLD modulation with task difficulty. In older adults, however, we observed an opposite structure-615 

function association. Since there was no age-related decline in the integrity of the IFOF tract (after accounting 616 

for WBFA), the association identified in older adults might involve modulating factor(s) beyond white matter 617

4.4 Limitations 624 

The current study was limited, by design, to WM tracts with “direct” connections between two functional 625 

clusters, but there might be functionally correlated regions that interact closely without structural connections 626 

between them (Hermundstad et al., 2013; Park & Friston, 2013). For example, indirect tracts such as the 627 

corpus callosum could be responsible for the interaction of BOLD modulation effects in different brain networks 628 

(or hemispheres). Relatedly, the current study focused on the contribution of each of the four white matter 629 

tracts connecting BOLD regions within- or across-networks on cognitive aging independently. However, 630 

additive or interactive effects in these tracts could impact cognition and will need future studies to explore. The 631 

current study is also limited by its cross-sectional design, which only permits identification of age effects on 632 

structure-function associations, rather than the effects of aging. The temporal ordering of these hypothesized 633 

associations can only be established through the analysis of within-person longitudinal data. 634 

5. CONCLUSIONS 635 

The current study utilized functionally-guided tractography derived from task-related functional 636 

activations in the multiple demand and default mode networks to examine whether white matter structure – 637 

function associations demonstrate compensatory aging effects on cognition, that is, an over recruitment might 638 

be linked to better cognitive performance. The results indicated that a single superficial white matter tract 639 

(fronto-insular U-fiber tract) in the multiple demand network demonstrated a significant age effect on its 640 

microstructure. By examining the quadratic effect of white matter FA, we found evidence that functional effects 641 

on executive function and fluid intelligence compensated for low integrity of the U-fiber tract. Consistent with 642 

our hypothesis, weakened functional activity in the DMN early in middle age coupled with increased activity in 643

Figure 1 . Conceptual hypothesis of structure-function association compensatory effect on cognitive performance . The nine ROIs in each brain are the functional clusters showing the effect of age-related BOLD modulation on task difficulty; the BOLD modulation legend illustrates the direction and strength of the effect where dark red/blue color indicates strengthened positive/negative BOLD modulation, respectively, while the lighter color represents weakened BOLD modulation effect as a result of brain aging. The four solid/dashed lines in each brain represent relative intact/degraded white matter connections from the hub of the multiple demand network (MDN), the insula, to the other four clusters in the MDN. The brain on the left represents the state of a healthy, younger brain, with relatively high white matter connectivity and high positive/negative BOLD modulation to task difficulty, both of which are linked to better performance. As the brain ages, white matter in the MDN degrades first (solid lines connecting red ROIs in younger adults become dashed lines with aging, on the brains on the right side). In response to this structural impairment in frontal-parietal regions, the functional activity could demonstrate either a “Non-compensatory” relation to cognition, where declines in BOLD modulation in both networks are associated with worse cognition, as the top right brain shows, or a “Compensatory” effect, where strengthened local activity (increased PosMod) coupled with weakened NegMod is linked to better cognition (bottom right brain).
