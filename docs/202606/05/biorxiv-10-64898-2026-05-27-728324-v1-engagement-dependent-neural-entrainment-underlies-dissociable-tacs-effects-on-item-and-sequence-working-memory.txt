Abstract 19 A central challenge in neuromodulation research is to elucidate how exogenous 20 electrical stimulation interacts with endogenous neural dynamics to shape cognitive 21 function. Drawing on empirical evidence for dissociable oscillatory mechanisms 22 underpinning item versus sequence working memory (WM), we employed transcranial 23 alternating current stimulation (tACS) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) to establish 24 a causal separation and characterize the underlying neurophysiological basis. We 25 observed a clear behavioral and neural dissociation: prefrontal 6-Hz theta stimulation 26 selectively enhanced item WM, whereas frontoparietal in-phase theta stimulation 27 specifically improved sequence WM. MEG further revealed task-specific modulation 28 effects: during sequence WM, where frontoparietal theta synchrony predicted memory 29 success, in-phase theta stimulation selectively amplified inter-regional synchronization. 30 In contrast, during item WM, where local theta power was indicative of memory 31 success, the identical stimulation overall exerted no significant modulation effect. 32 Critically, across both tasks and oscillatory measures (inter-regional synchronization and 33 theta power), the magnitude of stimulation-induced modulation scaled with each 34 region's or connection's contribution to memory performance, indicating that tACS 35 proportionately amplifies actively engaged oscillatory processes. This engagement-36 dependent principle explains task- and region-specific neurophysiological effects and 37 the variability in behavioral outcomes, providing a framework for designing precise, 38 functionally aligned stimulation protocols to enhance translational efficacy. 39 40

Introduction 46 Noninvasive brain stimulation serves as a potent tool to modulate neural activity and 47 probe the causal mechanisms of cognition. However, stimulation outcomes remain 48 highly variable across studies, tasks, and individuals (Booth et al., 2022; Imburgio & Orr, 49 2018; López-Alonso et al., 2014; Wiethoff et al., 2014). In particular, the efficacy of 50 transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS)—a method designed to interact with 51 and entrain endogenous neural rhythms (Antal & Paulus, 2013)—can vary significantly 52 even when delivered at the same frequency or targeting identical cortical regions 53 (Alekseichuk et al., 2017; Brauer et al., 2018; Kleinert et al., 2017; Rostami et al., 2021). 54 Evidence from domains such as selective attention, language processing, and large-55 scale cortical physiology indicates that stimulation effects can be region- and task-56 specific (Castrillon et al., 2020; Heinen et al., 2014; Meinzer et al., 2012). This 57 inconsistency highlights a fundamental theoretical challenge: how do externally applied 58 oscillatory fields interface with the brain's ongoing, task-dependent neural dynamics to 59 influence behavior? While state-dependent accounts propose that stimulation interacts 60 with the current functional state of the neural system (Alagapan et al., 2016; Bradley et 61 al., 2022), the field still lacks a mechanistic framework that explains why identical 62 stimulation parameters often produce variable effects across different tasks, individuals, 63 and contexts. Specifically, it remains unknown which neural processes are preferentially 64 modulated by tACS and how this modulation translates into task-specific behavioral 65 outcomes. Addressing this gap is the central aim of the present study. 66 67 Theta-band activity serves as an important test case for such a framework. Theta 68 oscillations are widely involved in working memory (WM), with frontal theta power 69 associated with maintaining item-specific content (Otstavnov et al., 2024; Proskovec et 70 al., 2018) and theta phase synchronization supporting the coordination of distributed 71 WM networks (Payne & Kounios, 2009; Sarnthein et al., 1998; Sauseng et al., 2010; Wu 72 et al., 2007). Recent evidence suggests that item and sequence WM may exert 73 differential demands on these theta mechanisms, even when recruiting overlapping 74 frontoparietal regions (Attout et al., 2019; Marshuetz et al., 2000; Rottschy et al., 2012). 75 Specifically, item WM has been linked to increases in local theta power (Brookes et al., 76

Direct 80 comparisons indicate that local theta and inter-regional theta phase synchronization 81 contribute partially dissociable to item versus sequence WM maintenance (Su et al., 82 2024), though the causal roles of these mechanisms remain to be determined. 83 84 tACS presents an opportunity to test these causal contributions by selectively 85 enhancing local oscillatory power (e.g., through unifocal theta stimulation) or promoting 86 inter-regional synchrony (e.g., via bifocal in-phase stimulation). Indeed, unifocal theta 87 tACS applied to the parietal or frontal cortex has been shown to enhance item WM 88 performance and increase theta power (Jaušovec et al., 2014; Pahor & Jaušovec, 2018; 89 Wolinski et al., 2018). In contrast, bifocal in-phase theta stimulation can strengthen 90 frontoparietal coherence and facilitate WM retention (Hu et al., 2022; Polanía et al., 91 2012), whereas anti-phase stimulation often disrupts performance and reduces 92 connectivity (Alekseichuk et al., 2017; Biel et al., 2022). Nevertheless, no 93 existing studies have examined the dissociated behavioral effects and the underlying 94 neurophysiological mechanisms of unifocal and bifocal stimulation for item and 95 sequence WM, which may aid in developing a unified framework for accounting for 96 these task- and region-specific effects. 97 98 The present study aims to establish such a framework in the context of WM. In addition 99 to identifying the dissociation of item and sequence WM, we utilized their partially 100 differentiable oscillatory signatures as a natural testbed for examining how tACS 101 interacts with task-engaged neural processes. In two controlled tACS experiments, we 102 applied unifocal prefrontal theta stimulation and bifocal frontoparietal in-phase or anti-103 phase theta stimulation while participants performed item and sequence WM tasks. In 104 experiment 3, we employed magnetoencephalography (MEG) to quantify how in-phase 105 theta stimulation modulates local theta power and frontoparietal theta phase 106 synchronization during item and sequence WM maintenance. First, if item WM relies primarily on local prefrontal theta power, 108 unifocal theta tACS should selectively enhance item WM, whereas bifocal in -phase 109 theta tACS—targeting frontoparietal synchronization—should selectively enhance 110 sequence WM. Second, if tACS interacts with task -engaged processes, the same 111 in -phase stimulation should increase frontoparietal theta synchronization only during 112 sequence WM, the task where this mechanism is behaviorally relevant, and not during 113 item WM. Third, and most critically, we predicted an engagement -dependent principle: 114 the magnitude of tACS -induced neural modulation should scale with each neural 115 process’s contribution to memory success, as indexed by the subsequent memory 116 effect. Our results provide a rigorous test of this principle, showing that externally 117 applied theta fields preferentially and proportionally enhance the oscillatory processes 118 actively supporting ongoing cognition. This framework moves beyond merely 119 demonstrating causal effects to explaining their variability and context dependence, 120 offering a mechanistic foundation for designing precise, functionally aligned stimulation 121 protocols. 122 123 

Results 124 In this study, we conducted three tACS experiments to investigate how exogenous 125 stimulation interacts with and modulates endogenous neural dynamics to influence 126 cognition, particularly within the context of WM (Fig. 1A). Experiment 1 enrolled 39 127 participants, who were randomly assigned to either the unifocal stimulation group (n=20) 128 or the bifocal stimulation group (n=19). Each participant completed three 129 counterbalanced experimental conditions across separate test days: the unifocal group 130 experienced 6 Hz stimulation, 35 Hz stimulation, and sham stimulation, while the bifocal 131 group received 6 Hz in-phase stimulation, 6 Hz anti-phase stimulation, and sham 132 stimulation (Fig. 1C). Stimulation frequency was set to 6ௗHz, a central theta frequency 133 (4–8ௗHz) known to support WM maintenance (Jensen & Tesche, 2002; Sauseng et al., 134 2005) and previously shown to be effective in tACS studies (Jaušovec et al., 2014; 135 Pahor & Jaušovec, 2018; Wolinski et al., 2018) . During the 20-minute tACS sessions, 136 followed by a 40-minute post-stimulation phase, all participants performed four WM 137 tasks, including two item WM tasks and two sequence WM tasks (Fig. 1B). The tACS effect on phase synchronization was modulated by task involvement 334 The above results demonstrate task-specific tACS effects: frontoparietal 6 Hz in-phase 335 tACS selectively enhanced theta phase synchronization during sequence WM task but 336 not during item WM task. A plausible explanation is that this modulation is contingent 337 upon task-specific neural engagement. Discussion 479 The present study tackles a central challenge in neuromodulation: understanding how 480 externally applied rhythmic stimulation interacts with the brain’s ongoing, task-engaged 481 activity to influence cognition. To explore this question, we combined tACS with 482 behavioral assays and MEG to investigate how identical stimulation interacts with two 483 WM tasks. These tasks activate overlapping frontoparietal regions but differ in their 484 reliance on local versus network-level theta mechanisms. Across both tasks, we found 485 convergent evidence for task-specific and engagement-dependent modulation of neural 486 activity and behavior. These results provide an integrative framework that explains 487 variability in stimulation outcomes and offers mechanistic insights into how external 488 oscillatory fields interface with endogenous brain dynamics. 489 490 A substantial body of research has demonstrated that the effects of noninvasive 491 stimulation depend on the brain’s current functional state (Alagapan et al., 2016; 492 Bradley et al., 2022). Stimulation does not uniformly affect cognitive contexts but 493 interacts differently with neural dynamics across various states, such as sleep phases 494 (Li et al., 2017; Massimini et al., 2005), awareness (Gersner et al., 2011), or attentional 495 engagement (Kamke et al., 2012). For instance, transcranial magnetic stimulation 496 (TMS) applied to the frontal eye field preferentially modulates attended networks during 497 selective attention (Heinen et al., 2014; Morishima et al., 2009) and high-frequency (20 498 Hz) repetitive TMS enhances memory-relevant networks during autobiographical 499 retrieval (Warren et al., 2019). Our study extends these observations, demonstrating 500 that stimulation outcomes can be significantly shaped by task requirements alone, even 501 when stimuli and timing are held constant. Specifically, we found that identical 502 frontoparietal in-phase tACS produced task-specific effects: stimulation selectively 503 enhanced sequence WM performance and increased frontoparietal theta 504 synchronization when such synchrony was behaviorally relevant, yet failed to exert a 505 significant effect when local oscillatory activity supported item WM maintenance. 506

Critically, we also found the in-phase theta stimulation differently modulated theta power 507 in the two WM tasks, in a manner consistent with theta contribution to item and 508 sequence WM. These findings provide compelling evidence to suggest that tACS 509 selectively modulates neural processes that are actively engaged by the current task, 510 even when stimulation parameters are held constant. 511 512 Importantly, the present study also refines our understanding of regional specificity in 513 neuromodulation. Previous research has shown that stimulation effects vary across 514 cortical regions (Castrillon et al., 2020) and depend on functional connectivity strength 515 within the targeted areas (Ezzyat et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2018; Solomon et al., 2018). 516 Our results further extend these findings by showing that regional susceptibility is 517 constrained by each region’s functional engagement during the task, quantified through 518 the SME. Specifically, stimulation-induced changes in frontoparietal synchronization 519 correlated with the regions’ synchronization SME, while changes in theta power 520 correlated with the regions’ theta SME. This suggests that stimulation does not merely 521 modulate the anatomical targets of the electric field but instead affects brain regions 522 actively contributing to task performance. 523 524 By pooling data from two tasks, we reveal a critical, engagement-dependent principle of 525 neuromodulation: externally applied rhythmic stimulation preferentially amplifies the 526 neural processes that actively support ongoing cognition, and does so in proportion to 527 their recruitment. This principle provides a unified explanation for why stimulation effects 528 are both task- and region-specific, and how identical stimulation parameters yield 529 distinct neurophysiological changes under different cognitive demands. Specifically, 530 when a neural process is strongly recruited by a task, it exhibits more substantial 531 stimulation-induced modulation; when engagement is low, stimulation effects are 532 correspondingly diminished. This principle held consistently across distinct oscillatory 533 measures, i.e., local theta power and inter-regional theta synchrony, and across both 534 WM tasks, underscoring its generality as an organizing rule for stimulation-brain 535 interactions. 536 537

The engagement-dependent principle has significant scientific and translational 538 implications. For example, stimulation protocols may need to be tailored to individuals 539 based on their neural engagement profiles, including personalized task difficulty, 540 stimulation timing, frequency, and targeted regions (Bueren et al., 2021; Fan et al., 541 2024; Soleimani et al., 2023). Moreover, rather than concentrating on presumed “poor 542 performance states”, closed-loop interventions may be more effective when delivered 543 during periods of strong engagement of the targeted mechanism, including during 544 optimal performance (Bergmann, 2018; Sitaram et al., 2017). Mechanistically, our 545 results help clarify the variability of stimulation outcomes reported in the 546 neuromodulation literature (Guerra et al., 2017; Homan et al., 2021): variation in the 547 engagement of the targeted mechanism, rather than inconsistency in stimulation 548 parameters, may be a key determinant of stimulation efficacy. 549 550 The selective emergence of frequency-matching benefits only during the sequence WM 551 task, but not the item WM task, provides further support for the engagement-dependent 552 principle. While the entrainment hypothesis predicts that tACS efficacy should increase 553 when stimulation frequency aligns with an individual's endogenous theta peak (Krause 554 et al., 2022), this mechanism alone cannot explain why the correlation between 555 frequency mismatch and dwPLI gain was absent in the item WM task. Instead, our 556 findings indicate that frequency matching confers a measurable benefit only when the 557 targeted neural process is actively engaged by the task. When the item WM task failed 558 to engage this same oscillatory mechanism, even a perfect frequency match produced 559 no measurable benefit. These findings do not refute frequency entrainment but rather 560 place it within a broader framework: entrainment is most effective when the targeted 561 neural process is already engaged by task demands. We therefore propose the 562 engagement-dependent resonance model, which posits that tACS efficacy arises from a 563 multiplicative interaction between (1) the alignment of stimulation frequency with 564 endogenous oscillatory dynamics and (2) the active cognitive engagement of the 565 relevant neural process. In this view, frequency entrainment provides the potential for 566 modulation, but task engagement serves as the gate that realizes this potential. 567 568

In contrast, sequence WM depends on theta phase coupling across 574 distributed brain regions to maintain ordered representations (Johnson et al., 2018; Su 575 et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2021), with frontoparietal theta phase synchronization 576 increases with memory load and cognitive demand (Payne & Kounios, 2009; Sauseng 577 et al., 2005; Schack et al., 2005)—a pattern indicative of executive control recruitment 578 (Mizuhara & Yamaguchi, 2007). Inter-regional theta phase synchronization is further 579 enhanced during tasks requiring integration of objects with temporal or spatial contexts 580 (Benchenane et al., 2010; Jones & Wilson, 2005; Kay, 2005). Building on the seminal 581 work of Polanía et al. (2012), which demonstrated that in-phase bifocal tACS enhances 582 WM by promoting frontoparietal theta synchronization, the present study addresses why 583 and under what conditions such stimulation is effective. By directly comparing two WM 584 tasks with dissociable theta mechanisms, we show that identical in-phase stimulation 585 produces task-specific effects, and that the magnitude of neural modulation scales with 586 task engagement. These findings provide a mechanistic account of variability in tACS 587 outcomes and introduce the engagement-dependent principle as a unifying framework. 588 589 Several methodological choices strengthened our conclusions. First, our design used 590 identical stimuli, timing, and stimulation parameters across both tasks, thereby 591 eliminating alternative explanations. Peripheral sensations (e.g., tingling, phosphenes) 592 and E-field differences between stimulation conditions were matched across tasks and 593 thus cannot account for the task-specific effects. Consequently, the only remaining 594 factor driving the differential tACS effects between sequence and item WM tasks is the 595 task requirement itself. Second, individualizing task difficulty across participants allowed 596 more precise estimation of neural engagement. Third, MEG provided high temporal and 597 spatial resolution for characterizing oscillatory dynamics and their modulation by tACS, 598 which is crucial for examining region-specific oscillatory mechanisms. Non-parametric cluster-based permutation test 846 The non-parametric cluster-based permutation test (Maris & Oostenveld, 2007) was 847 applied to identify clusters of time-frequency power exhibiting reliable differences 848 between remembered and forgotten trials. This procedure sums neighboring t-values 849 exceeding a cluster-forming threshold and compares the resulting cluster sizes to a null 850 distribution of maximal cluster sums derived from Monte-Carlo label swapping. 851 Specifically, the empirical cluster t-value (remembered vs. forgotten) was calculated by 852 summing neighboring t-values above a two-sided alpha threshold (0.025), with 853 neighboring channels defined via FieldTrip’s triangulation method (minimum cluster 854 size: 3 channels). The cluster-level t-value represented the sum of all within-cluster t-855 values, which was compared against a null distribution of 1000 surrogate t-values 856 generated by random label permutations (two-sided alpha: 0.025). Corrected 857 significance was determined by ranking the empirical t-value against the surrogate 858 distribution. 859 860
